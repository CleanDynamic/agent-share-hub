Motion audit · design-motion-principles

buildgallery.ai — twelve prompts of motion, read as one system

A pass over every transition, keyframe and scroll in the product, run at the end of a thirty-two-prompt visual remodel. The remodel added motion in a dozen separate prompts and never once looked at the result together — which is precisely the condition that produces scattered, AI-slop motion. This is what looking at it together found.

Overall

The motion here was never badly judged — almost every individual choice is defensible. It was never decided. Eleven durations and five easings had accumulated across 278 files, each one arrived at by whichever prompt happened to be writing that surface, so the only answer to "how fast is a hover in this product" was "open the file and see". That is the slop signature: not one wrong value, but no system. Nine findings, seven of them mechanical and now fixed, two of them about where the product should spend motion it currently does not.

01

How each practitioner reads the motion

Weighted for what this product is: a gallery you browse and a workspace you work in, sharing one component kit. The reading half can afford a little pacing; the working half can afford none, and the theme's own motion rules are already written in Emil's voice — ≤200ms, transform and opacity, never ease-in on UI.

Where the timings land

Every distinct duration the product used, before and after. The zones describe the cost of a duration, not a rule: under 100ms reads as instant, 100–300ms as responsive, 300–500ms as deliberate, beyond that as slow. The remodel's spread is the finding.

transition: all, 83 times, in three spellings

What50 as Tailwind's transition-all and 33 as transition: "all 0.15s" inside inline style objects. The second group is the interesting one: it survived a first pass aimed squarely at removing it, because the guard's own regex required all to follow the colon directly and there was a quote in the way.

Why it mattersall subscribes to every property the element will ever have. It is not a shorthand for "the ones I'm changing" — it is a standing instruction to animate whatever a future class adds, including the layout properties. Six of these were already quietly easing width or max-height because something downstream had started setting them.

Recommended motionName the properties. feedback() returns the five a control actually changes — background-color, border-color, color, opacity, transform — at 150ms on the product's curve, and its parameter is a closed union, so box-shadow and the layout properties are a build error rather than a review comment.

CriticalEmil

26 animated layout properties, six of them over 400ms

WhatTwelve progress and XP bars easing width over 400–700ms, four disclosure panels easing max-height or height, four toggle thumbs and a crop box easing left and top, and four arbitrary transition-[width,height,padding] lists in the shadcn sidebar.

Why it mattersEach of these invalidates layout for its whole subtree on every frame of the animation. A 700ms width tween on the XP bar is 42 forced reflows of the rail it sits in, for a bar whose value the reader has already been told in text beside it.

Recommended motionNone. The usual advice — animate transform: scaleX() instead — is not available here: this project's review rules forbid changing width, height or position on an existing layout element, and a pill-shaped bar distorts under scaleX anyway. The tween goes and the value stays, which is what the theme's "never animate a layout property" actually asks for.

Ten animated box-shadows, four of them the same effect rewritten

WhatA deep link landing, two block references jumping to their block, and a bounty solve arriving — four hand-rolled "look here" rings, none aware of the others, three easing ease-in on the way out. Plus six hover and selection shadows, and one hiding inside a @keyframes where a scanner reading transition: could never see it.

Why it mattersbox-shadow is not compositable. Transitioning it re-rasterises the element on every frame — and the theme's own comment about this is on the gallery card, which has twenty-four instances in a grid. The four duplicate rings are the more telling half: four prompts each solved the same problem without knowing the others had.

Recommended motionOne pulseRing in the motion module, drawn with an outline: the same ring, no part in layout, and a colour that composites. Where a shadow genuinely changes on hover it moves to a pseudo-element whose opacity is animated — the pattern the gallery card already proved.

CriticalJakub

Reduced motion reached three selectors; hover reached none

WhatTwo problems with one shape. prefers-reduced-motion was honoured on three CSS selectors and in eight modules — the streak flame, the skill tree, the typing indicator, the XP bar and everything four dependencies bring kept moving. And of 563 Tailwind hover: utilities plus 13 hand-written :hover rules, exactly two were gated on a fine pointer.

Why it mattersA reader who asked for stillness and got it on five surfaces out of forty has not been answered. On the hover side, a touch device makes :hover stick after a tap, so every ungated hover leaves a control looking permanently pressed until something else is touched — on a product whose primary surface is a grid of tappable cards.

Recommended motionOne rule in index.css over *, ::before and ::after, collapsing durations to 0.01ms rather than zero — a cancelled transition never fires transitionend, so anything waiting on that event to unmount a node would hang, for exactly the readers least able to work around it. For hover, Tailwind's hoverOnlyWhenSupported compiles all 563 utilities into the same media query: one decision instead of 563.

ImportantJheyEmil

Delight spent on ambient loops instead of on the moments that earn it

WhatTen infinite animations. Four are decoration announcing something the colour already said: a skill node's "available" ring throbbing every 2s, the connector between two unlocked skills at 2.4s, the streak flame at 1.4s, and a sheen sweeping the XP bar forever. Three of the four sit in persistent chrome, where they never stop.

Why it mattersThis is the anti-checklist's first category, and the reason it says flag any instance: a pulse is a claim on attention, and a claim that is always being made is noise. The skill tree can show a dozen at once. Meanwhile the real moments — the reproduction landing, the bounty being solved — get nothing.

Recommended motionRemove all four; the ring, the fill and the glyph colour already carry the state. The six loops that stay are the ones where the movement is the information: a spinner, two skeleton sweeps, the typing indicator, the text caret, and the canvas snap point that exists only while something is being dragged onto it.

Hover answered by growing things, in six components

WhatEight hover:scale-*, three of them byte-identical on three quest CTAs, the rest spread across a colour swatch, a star rating, two emoji pickers and a glass card that grew to 1.02.

Why it mattersThe anti-checklist's threshold is three distinct components sharing the same scale with no discriminating context; this is six. Scale-on-hover is the reflex answer, and a product that already has a hover vocabulary — colour, opacity, and a 1px lift — reaching for it anyway is a sign the vocabulary was not known rather than not liked.

Recommended motionSpend the vocabulary. The CTAs take the colour change they already had; the four small pick targets take opacity, which says "this one" just as clearly at a fraction of the compositing cost and does not shift a neighbour's optical alignment.

The build page — the product's most-read surface — arrived all at once

WhatThe theme permits a scroll entry on exactly two surfaces. The gallery grid had one, hand-rolled with its own three constants. The build page, which is the thing the gallery exists to send people to, had none.

Why it mattersThis is the one place in the product where pacing is not decoration: a build page is a document with a provenance line, a header and a set of tabs, read top to bottom. Nothing else should get this — an app surface that withholds its content until you scroll to it is arguing with the person trying to use it — but this surface earns it.

Recommended motion450ms opacity plus a 14px rise, IntersectionObserver, once, disconnecting on the first intersection. No stagger: a page's sections are already separated in time by the reader's own scrolling. The hook fails towards visible — under reduced motion, without IntersectionObserver, or before the effect runs, the element is already in its final state, because an element stuck at opacity 0 is worse than no animation.

Which lens carried this audit

Emil Kowalski — Restraint & Speed

Every critical finding was a cost finding: properties that cannot be composited, layout invalidated on every frame, all subscribing to things nobody chose. That is Emil's territory, and it is also, almost word for word, what this product's own theme document already said — which is the most useful thing this audit found. The rules were written. Nothing enforced them, so twelve prompts each re-derived motion from scratch and the result drifted exactly as far as you would expect. Jakub's lens named the gaps between surfaces; Jhey's named the two moments worth spending on next.

  • Lean EmilTreat the duration list as closed. If a surface wants a twelfth value, that is a conversation about the vocabulary, not a number to type into a component.
  • Lean JakubAssume any rule without an assertion behind it is already broken somewhere. Three of the four critical findings here were rules the codebase had written down and stopped following.
  • Lean JheySpend the celebration budget on the reproduction and the solved gap. Both are rare, both are earned, and the particle machinery is already built.
